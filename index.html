Article review · Energy and Buildings, 2024

Combining physical laws and ANN to predict data center cooling energy

Xuezhi Li, Xinyi Wang, Zhiguang He, Xiaoxuan Chen & Zhen Li
Energy and Buildings, 311, Article 114170 · doi.org/10.1016/j.enbuild.2024.114170

Presented by Group 2
University of Ottawa
This paper asks a practical question: how can we predict how much electricity a data center's cooling system will use, accurately and in a way engineers can still trust? The authors' answer is to let physics do most of the work and let a neural network fix what physics gets wrong.
Context

Why predict cooling energy?

~40%

of a data center's total electricity is commonly estimated to go to cooling — the largest load after the IT equipment itself.

  • Every watt of computing becomes a watt of heat that must be removed.
  • Accurate forecasts let operators optimize setpoints, schedule loads and plan capacity.
  • Predictions also underpin digital twins, model-predictive control and fault detection.
  • Better prediction → lower PUE, lower cost, lower emissions.
2
Cooling is the biggest overhead in a data center. If you can predict its energy use well, you can control it well. That is the motivation for the whole paper.
The problem

Two modeling traditions, two weaknesses

Physics-based · "white box"

Thermodynamics & heat-transfer equations

Interpretable — every term has a physical meaning

Generalizes to conditions not seen in data

Simplifying assumptions miss nonlinear, real-world effects

Slow to build and calibrate for each system

Data-driven · "black box"

Artificial neural network (ANN)

Learns complex nonlinear relationships directly from data

Fast to evaluate once trained

Little interpretability

Accuracy limited by the amount and variety of training data; weak outside it

The core challenge: balancing accuracy against interpretability.

3
Physics models are trustworthy but approximate. Neural networks are flexible but opaque and only as good as their training data. The paper tries to get the best of both.
Experimental setup

Test bed: an integrated heat pipe cooling system

  • Laboratory data center cooled by an integrated heat pipe cooling (IHPC) system, built on the research group's earlier work.
  • Heat pipes move heat passively when outdoor air is cold (free / natural cooling); vapor-compression refrigeration takes over or assists when it is warm.
  • Measured operating parameters and the system's metered energy consumption form the dataset.

Mode switching with weather and load is exactly the kind of nonlinearity a simple physical model struggles to capture.

4
The test system is an integrated heat pipe cooling system in a lab data center. It switches between passive heat-pipe cooling, a combined mode and conventional compressor cooling depending on outdoor temperature. That switching makes energy use highly nonlinear.
Method

Three models, same data

Model 1

Physical model

Heat-transfer and energy-consumption equations for the cooling system's components, calibrated to the rig.

Model 2

Pure ANN

A neural network trained to map operating inputs directly to energy consumption.

Model 3 · the contribution

Combined model

Physical model provides the baseline prediction; an ANN learns and corrects its errors.

All three were trained and tested separately on the measured laboratory data so their predictive accuracy could be compared head-to-head.

5
To make a fair comparison, the authors build all three models on the same measured data: physics alone, neural network alone, and the combined approach.
The key idea

Let the ANN learn what physics gets wrong

Training: run the physical model, compare with the metered power, and compute the residual r = P_meas − P_phys. The ANN is trained to predict r from the operating inputs.

Prediction: no meter needed — the ANN estimates the error for new conditions and that correction is added to the physical model's output.

6
This is the heart of the paper. Physics gives a baseline. During training, we compare that baseline to what the meter actually measured; the difference is the residual. The neural network learns to predict that residual from the same inputs. When we later predict, we add the network's estimated error to the physics output. The physics guarantees sensible behavior; the network sharpens accuracy.

Qualitative summary of the authors' conclusions. See the paper's results section for the exact error metrics; the work cites ASHRAE Guideline 14, the standard benchmark for calibrated energy models.

7
Across the tests, the combined model generally gave the best accuracy. Importantly it keeps the interpretability of the physics model, because the network only nudges the physics output rather than replacing it.
Interpretation

Why the hybrid works

  • Easier learning task. The ANN learns a small correction, not the whole physics — so it needs less data.
  • Prior knowledge built in. Thermodynamics constrains the output; the network can't wander far from physically plausible values.
  • Division of labour. Physics ⇒ generalization. ANN ⇒ the nonlinear details physics simplifies away.
How it relates

Residual hybrid vs. PINNs

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) embed equations inside the network's loss function.

This paper instead uses a serial "base + correction" structure: simpler to build, and the physics model stays usable on its own.

8
Why does this help? Learning a small correction is much easier than learning the whole system from scratch. And because the physics is always in the loop, predictions stay physically sensible. This differs from physics-informed neural networks, which bake equations into training; here the two models stay separate and are simply added together.
Critical view

Limitations to keep in mind

  • Single laboratory system. Results come from one integrated heat pipe rig; performance in a full-scale, commercial data center is untested.
  • Random train/test split. The authors note the data was split randomly. For time-series operation data, a chronological split is a tougher, more realistic test.
  • Physics still required. A reasonable physical model must exist first; the hybrid inherits any structural errors the ANN can't learn.
  • Correction is data-bound. Outside the training range, the ANN's error estimate may be unreliable — though the physics baseline limits the damage.
9
A few caveats. It is one lab system. The random split may make results look better than they would on genuinely future data. And you still need to build a physics model first, which is the time-consuming part the paper hopes to ease.
Conclusion

Takeaways

1 · Don't choose — combine

Physics and AI are complementary: one brings understanding, the other brings flexibility.

2 · Learn the error

Training an ANN on the physics model's residual gave the best overall accuracy while keeping interpretability.

3 · Toward smarter control

Trustworthy, accurate predictions are a building block for digital twins and energy-optimal cooling control.

Embedding prior physical knowledge into machine learning is a practical path to models engineers can both trust and rely on.

Three takeaways: combine physics with AI instead of choosing; let the network learn the physics model's mistakes; and use these trustworthy predictions for better control of data center cooling.
